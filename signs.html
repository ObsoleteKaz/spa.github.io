Part 1

What to notice, in yourself or someone else

None of these prove anything on their own. Together, or paired with a gut feeling that something's off, they're worth taking seriously.

Warning signs rarely arrive as one dramatic moment. More often they build slowly — a friend who used to text back within the hour goes quiet for days, a coworker who was reliable starts missing things, a family member's jokes about "not being around much longer" start landing a little too flat to laugh off. None of the ten signs below is, by itself, proof that someone is suicidal. But people who are struggling this seriously usually show more than one of them, and the signs tend to cluster and intensify rather than appear in isolation. The goal of this page isn't to turn you into a diagnostician — it's to make the pattern recognizable enough that you trust the feeling of "something's not right" instead of talking yourself out of it.

Withdrawing

Pulling away from friends, family, or things they used to care about. Going quiet in a way that feels different from their normal quiet. This is often one of the earliest signs and the easiest to miss, because it can look like someone is simply busy, tired, or going through a normal introverted phase — the difference is usually duration and how out of character it feels to people who know them well.

Talking about being a burden

Saying, even in passing, that people would be "better off" without them, or that there's no point going on. These comments are frequently dropped casually, almost as throwaway lines, precisely because saying them directly feels too heavy. Treat the casual delivery as no reason to treat the content casually.

Giving things away

Suddenly settling affairs, giving away possessions, or saying goodbye in ways that feel oddly final. This can look mundane — handing over a favorite jacket, paying back a small debt unprompted, tying up loose ends that don't urgently need tying up — but the timing and tone are usually what set it apart from ordinary generosity.

A sudden calm after a low period

This can look like relief, and sometimes genuinely is. But for some people, a sudden lift in mood follows the decision to act, not a resolution of the underlying pain — the relief comes from having made a choice, not from the choice being a good one. It's worth checking in rather than assuming things have simply improved.

Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy

Sleeping far more or far less than usual, eating noticeably more or less, or a flatness of energy that doesn't lift with rest. Not proof of anything on its own — these overlap with ordinary stress and many other conditions — but combined with the signs above, worth paying attention to rather than writing off as "a rough week."

Increased use of alcohol or drugs

Sometimes a way of numbing pain that isn't being talked about anywhere else. A noticeable increase in how much or how often someone is using, especially alone, can be a sign they've found no other outlet for what they're carrying.

Talking or joking about death

Not every dark joke is a warning sign — plenty of people use gallows humor without any suicidal intent. But a pattern of it, especially paired with hopelessness or said in a flat, unamused tone, is worth a direct, gentle question rather than a shrug or a change of subject.

Feeling trapped or like a burden

Phrases like "there's no way out," "I can't do this anymore," or "everyone would be fine without me" describe a specific kind of pain, not just a bad mood. People often say these things once and then never bring it up again unless someone follows up — take them at face value the first time.

Losing interest in the future

Not making plans anymore — for next week, next year, anything — can be as telling as anything they say out loud. Watch for someone who used to talk about upcoming trips, goals, or milestones suddenly going quiet on all of it, as if the future has stopped being something they picture themselves in.

Extreme mood swings

Sharp, unexplained shifts between despair and unusual calm or agitation, especially over a short period, are worth paying attention to. These swings can be confusing to witness — someone seems fine one hour and inconsolable the next — and that unpredictability is itself part of the signal.

If you notice these

One sign isn't a diagnosis — but don't wait for certainty

You don't need to be sure before you say something. You don't need a clinical vocabulary or the perfect opening line. A plain, direct question — "I've noticed you've seemed really low lately, are you okay?" — is enough to open the door. The How to Help page walks through what tends to help once that door is open, and what to avoid.
