Part 2

If you're worried about someone

You don't need the right words. You need to show up, ask directly, and not leave them to handle it alone.

Most people freeze up before a conversation like this, worried they'll say the wrong thing and make it worse. In practice, the risk runs the other way: staying silent out of fear does far more damage than an imperfect attempt to help. You don't need to be a counselor. You need to notice out loud, ask directly, and not disappear afterward.

What helps

  • Ask directly. "Are you thinking about suicide?" does not plant the idea — it opens the door.
  • Listen without rushing to fix it. Let them finish sentences, even the uncomfortable ones.
  • Stay with them, or help them reach a hotline or counselor, rather than leaving them to do it alone.
  • Follow up later. One conversation rarely fixes everything, but it can be the start.
  • Take practical steps seriously — if they mention a specific plan or means, treat that as urgent, not as venting.
  • Be patient with silences. Not every gap needs to be filled by you.

What to avoid

  • Don't promise secrecy. If someone is in danger, other people may need to know.
  • Don't minimize ("it's not that bad") or compare their pain to others'.
  • Don't wait for a "better time." If it feels urgent, treat it as urgent.
  • Don't try to handle a crisis entirely alone — bring in a professional or a hotline.
  • Don't argue them out of how they feel, even with good intentions. Debating the logic of it rarely helps in the moment.
  • Don't disappear after the first conversation. Silence afterward can feel like the concern wasn't real.

Starting the conversation

You don't need a perfect opener. Naming what you've noticed and asking directly works better than hinting: "You haven't seemed like yourself lately — I'm worried about you. Are you thinking about suicide?" Being specific about the word "suicide" matters; euphemisms can leave room for someone to downplay what they're actually experiencing, and asking plainly signals that you can handle hearing the real answer.

If they say yes

Stay calm and stay present — your reaction sets the tone for whether they keep talking. Ask if they have a plan or access to a means of harming themselves; a specific plan combined with access is more urgent than thoughts without either. Don't leave them alone if you believe they're in immediate danger — help them contact a crisis line, a trusted person, or emergency services, and stay with them while they do it rather than handing off the number and stepping away.

After the conversation

One talk rarely resolves everything. Check in again in a day, a week, a month — a short message ("thinking of you, how are you doing") tells someone the concern wasn't a one-time performance. If you're supporting someone long-term, look after your own wellbeing too; you can't pour from an empty cup, and it's fine to loop in other trusted people to share the load rather than carrying it alone yourself.
