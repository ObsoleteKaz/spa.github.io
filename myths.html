Part 4

Common misconceptions, and what's actually true

A lot of well-meaning people stay quiet or say the wrong thing because of ideas about suicide that simply aren't accurate. Clearing these up costs nothing and can genuinely change how a conversation goes.

Misinformation about suicide isn't just wrong — it actively changes how people act. Someone who believes asking directly "plants the idea" stays silent when a direct question could have opened a door. Someone who believes attempts are just for attention dismisses a real warning sign as a performance. Each row below pairs a belief that's still common with what the evidence actually shows.

The myth

Asking someone if they're thinking about suicide will put the idea in their head.

The fact

Research consistently shows the opposite — asking directly does not increase risk, and often brings real relief to someone who's been holding it in alone.

The myth

People who talk about it are just seeking attention and won't actually act on it.

The fact

Most people who die by suicide gave some kind of warning beforehand. Talking about it should always be taken seriously, not dismissed.

The myth

Once someone is determined to do it, there's nothing anyone can do to stop them.

The fact

Suicidal crises are often short-lived and tied to a specific moment. Support, distance from the crisis, and access to help genuinely change outcomes.

The myth

It only affects people with a diagnosed mental illness.

The fact

Many people who experience suicidal thoughts have no formal diagnosis at all. It can be tied to loss, isolation, stress, or a crisis that feels inescapable in the moment.

The myth

If someone seems better after a rough patch, the danger has passed.

The fact

A sudden lift in mood can be a genuine recovery — but it can also follow a decision. It's worth a gentle check-in rather than assuming.

The myth

Bringing it up is none of my business, and I could make things worse by getting involved.

The fact

You don't need training or the perfect words. Showing you've noticed, and that you care, is rarely the wrong move — staying silent out of fear usually helps no one.

The myth

Suicide happens without any warning — there's nothing anyone could have noticed beforehand.

The fact

Most people show some combination of warning signs beforehand — withdrawal, hopelessness, talk of being a burden. They're often missed or explained away, not absent.

The myth

People who survive an attempt were never really serious about it.

The fact

Surviving doesn't mean the crisis wasn't real. It means help arrived, or the moment passed — and it's a chance to get real support, not proof the pain wasn't genuine.
