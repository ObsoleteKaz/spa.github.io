If this page is for you right now

You don't have to have the words figured out.

If you're struggling, this part of the site is for you — not just for the people worried about you. You don't need a plan or a tidy explanation to reach out. You just need to reach out.

If you're reading this because things feel unbearable right now, none of what follows is about fixing your whole situation today. It's about getting through the next stretch of time — the next hour, the next conversation — because the version of things that feels permanent right now usually isn't.

It's okay to not be okay

Struggling doesn't mean you're broken or weak. It means something is genuinely hard right now, and hard things deserve real support — not just willpower. A lot of people who reach this point have spent a long time trying to push through on their own, and that effort deserves recognition even if it hasn't been enough.

Tell one person, even badly

You don't need the right words. "I'm not doing okay" or "I don't feel safe" is enough to start with. Say it to a friend, a family member, a teacher, or a hotline — whoever feels safest. It's fine if it comes out clumsy, or if you cry, or if you can't finish the sentence the first time you try.

Keep a little structure

Eating something, however small, and getting some sleep and daylight, won't fix things — but they make the next hour slightly easier to get through. When everything feels overwhelming, the goal isn't a full routine, it's the smallest possible version of one.

Put some distance between you and the decision

Feelings that feel permanent right now often aren't. If you can, wait — call someone, go to a different room, get through the next ten minutes before anything else. If you've been thinking about a specific way you might hurt yourself, put physical distance between yourself and it: hand it to someone else, lock it away, leave the space. Buying time is a real strategy, not a stalling tactic.

Professional support helps more than people expect

A counselor or doctor isn't just for emergencies. Talking to someone trained for this, even once, can make things feel more manageable than carrying it alone. It's not a sign you've failed at handling things yourself — it's what that kind of training exists for.

You're allowed to ask for help more than once

If the first person or hotline you reach doesn't help the way you needed, that's not a dead end — try again, or try someone else. Persistence here is not weakness. Different people and services respond differently; one unhelpful conversation says nothing about whether the next one will help.

Make it slightly harder to act on the worst moment

If you've thought about how you might hurt yourself, put time and distance between you and that method right now — hand something to someone else, lock it away, leave the room. This isn't about willpower, it's about buying yourself time, and time is often what changes the outcome.

You are not a burden for needing this

The thought that you're a weight on the people around you is a common symptom of what you're going through, not an accurate read on how they feel. People who care about you would almost always rather be interrupted, inconvenienced, or woken up at 2am than lose you — even if that's hard to believe right now.

A few things that are true

What tends to help, even a little

Crises like this one are often more time-limited than they feel in the moment — the urge to act on suicidal thoughts, even an intense one, typically rises and falls rather than staying at the same pitch forever. Getting through the next hour, then the next, is a real strategy, not a small one. So is telling someone, even if it feels awkward or like too much. Most people, when told plainly, want to help and don't know it's needed until they're told. You don't owe anyone a polished explanation of what you're feeling before you're allowed to ask for support.

Right now

If it feels urgent, treat it as urgent

You don't have to be at the worst possible point to reach out — but if you are, please don't wait it out alone. Call or text 988 (US), use the chat at 988lifeline.org, or find your country's line on the resources page.
